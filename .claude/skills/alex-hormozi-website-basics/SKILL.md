# Alex Hormozi website basics

> **Companion to the web-design set — you are in the conversion-*principles* layer.** This skill owns *which few decisions actually move a conversion rate, and how to reason about them*. Reach for a sibling when the job shifts:
> - **landing-pages** — the section-by-section blueprint: page order, social-proof rules, form design, cold-traffic structure. Use it once you know *what* to fix and need the *layout*.
> - **stunning-websites** — visual execution: type systems, palettes, motion, avoiding the AI-generated look.
> - **demo-sites** — building a spec site to win a client.
>
> Overlap with landing-pages is intentional and they agree; this skill is the shorter, principle-first lens for diagnosis and prioritisation.

Distilled from a web-design breakdown of Alex Hormozi's conversion advice, by an
agency operator who has built 800+ sites. The through-line: **traffic is an
auction you can lose, conversion is an asset you own.**

> "The prices are going to only consistently go up, and most platforms are based
> on an auction — multiple businesses competing for the same eyeballs. The people
> who are better able to convert those eyeballs are going to outcompete and win."
> — Neil Patel, quoted by Hormozi

Everyone obsesses over traffic. Almost nobody obsesses over what happens when
that traffic lands. That gap is the single most expensive thing business owners
get wrong — money poured into driving people to a page that was never
purpose-built to convert them.

## Rule 0 — Pretty and converting are different products

A beautiful award-winning site loses to a plain one over and over, because the
plain one was **clear** and the pretty one made you work to understand it.

If a visitor cannot tell **what you do** and **how to take the next step**
within a few seconds, the design has failed at its one job no matter how good
it looks.

When someone asks for a "redesign", diagnose before you agree to one. Most
redesigns change 40 things, teach the owner nothing, and move conversion by
accident. Ask what the page is failing at first.

## Rule 1 — Fewest variables, biggest difference

> "The people who make the best decisions focus on the fewest variables that
> make the biggest difference."

The win is making **two or three decisions that move conversion** and
deliberately ignoring the rest. Most people do the opposite: rebuild everything,
change nothing that mattered.

**How this shows up in your work:** when a user hands you an underperforming
site, resist the full-page rewrite. Name the two or three highest-leverage
changes, say explicitly what you are *not* touching and why, and make those.

## Rule 2 — One split test a week

> "Every week we have our marketing meeting and we do one split test. Just one.
> Everyone wants to test 100 things — this forces us to be very focused on the
> one thing we think will drive the highest result."

Slow is smooth and smooth is fast. One deliberate change at a time is the only
way to learn what actually moved the needle. Forty simultaneous changes produce
a number you cannot attribute to anything.

Practical cadence: a standing weekly slot, one test, winner vs. control, and the
winning pattern rolls out to every other page.

**Caveat worth stating to the user:** true statistical significance needs real
traffic (roughly 40–50k visitors/month for micro-tests). Below that, still change
one thing at a time — but judge it on best-practice reasoning and cohort trends,
and prefer bigger, more decisive changes over button-colour tweaks.

## Rule 3 — Almost all the juice is above the fold

> "We simply split test the headlines on our landing pages. That's it. The next
> thing is the image underneath the headline. Almost all of the juice — the
> biggest jumps in conversion rate — happens above the fold."

The first screen before any scroll is **the only part of your site you can be
certain every visitor sees**, and realistically most will never scroll past it.
If you get one thing right on the entire website, make it this.

Nobody reads a website. They skim a headline, glance at an image, and their
brain decides in about half a second whether they are in the right place. That
half-second judgment then **colours everything below it** — if the top looks
cheap, they read your best testimonial with doubt and find reasons not to buy
without knowing why.

So test what Hormozi tests, in this order:

### 1. The headline

Must make clear who you are, what you do, and — above all — **what the visitor
gets out of it**, in five seconds or less. That last part is what everyone
forgets, and it matters far more than clever wording.

- Sell the end state, not the thing you hand over (see the Value Equation below).
- Clear beats clever, every time. Long-but-clear beats short-but-vague.
- If a stranger can't repeat back what you do after one read, it's not done.

### 2. The image directly beneath it

**Swap out the stock photo.** Everyone can spot one — the team high-fiving in a
boardroom nobody believes is your office. The moment they clock it, a small voice
asks *"what else on this site is fake?"*

Real imagery — even slightly imperfect real imagery — does the opposite. With AI
flooding the internet with perfect, soulless design, **something that looks like a
real human touched it is now the strongest trust signal available.** Use the actual
product, the actual work, the actual people, the actual premises.

Budget your effort accordingly: the hero deserves the majority of the attention
on the whole page.

## The Value Equation — the framework that should run the whole page

```
              DREAM OUTCOME  ×  PERCEIVED LIKELIHOOD OF ACHIEVEMENT
   VALUE  =  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
                  TIME DELAY  ×  EFFORT & SACRIFICE
```

Raise the top, shrink the bottom. Hardly anyone builds a website around this.
Here is each term translated into web design.

### Dream outcome → your headline and copy

What the customer actually wants, which is **never your product**. Nobody wakes
up wanting to buy a website; they want more leads. Nobody wants a renovation;
they want the room that impresses everyone who walks in.

Write the end state, not the feature list of the thing you're handing over. Use
the "so that" chain to get there: *exterior cleaning → so that solar panels
produce more → so that the power bill drops.*

### Perceived likelihood → your proof

How much they believe that if they buy, they'll actually get the outcome.

> "Amazon sells the same products as everyone else, literally. They increased the
> perceived likelihood of achievement by adding all the reviews."

Amazon didn't change the product — they changed **how sure you felt buying it.**
On your site that is:

- Testimonials and case studies with real names, faces, and sources
- Real numbers and specific results
- A clear guarantee (see risk reversal below)
- Anything that shows the outcome rather than claiming it — before/after, video

### Time delay → your speed

People hear "speed" and think delivery. On a website, speed starts long before
that: **how fast the page loads, and how fast someone can understand what you
do.** A slow, cluttered page adds time and effort before the visitor has bought
anything — you lose the equation before they've even scrolled.

> "Speed is one of the best all-time competitive advantages."

Also sell the real time-to-value where you honestly can: "leads in 30 days",
"live in two weeks", "reply within one business hour".

### Effort & sacrifice → your friction

Every extra field, extra step, extra thing to decipher, extra click. Cut what
isn't critical to conversion. Fewer form fields, fewer steps in the process,
plainer language, one obvious next action.

### The counter-intuitive part — where the money actually is

> "Most people, myself included, spent the first half to two-thirds of my career
> trying to beef up the top side — promise bigger, more testimonials, more
> results. But the actual fortunes rely on the **bottom side** of the equation."

Everyone pours their energy into the promise. The money is in **making people
believe it, and making it fast and effortless.** On a website: the headline gets
them to look; proof, speed and low friction get them to act.

When you audit a page, expect to find the top half over-invested and the bottom
half nearly empty. That's the opportunity.

## You are selling against inaction, not competitors

> "Most people think they're selling against their competition. I disagree. What
> you're really selling against is inaction. You're selling against doubt, against
> risk. The comfort of not doing the thing is so high — we have to lower the
> action threshold so much that there's so little friction, they buy."

The thing you're actually up against is the visitor closing the tab and telling
themselves *maybe later*. What keeps them doing that is risk — the nagging fear
that it won't work **for them specifically**.

So a real chunk of the page has exactly one job: **lower that fear until clicking
the button is the easy choice.**

### Risk reversal

- Make the guarantee **specific**. The vague "satisfaction guaranteed" badge is
  precisely the kind every brain skips past. Spell out exactly what happens if it
  doesn't work — what they get back, when, and how.
- Not every business can offer a strong guarantee. If you can, make it
  unmissable, top to bottom.
- Where a guarantee isn't possible, substitute other risk-killers: free first
  step, no card required, no-sales-pitch promise, cancel anytime, fixed price,
  named response time.

### Placement matters as much as content

Put trust elements **right around the call-to-action button**, deliberately.
That is the exact spot where doubt spikes — the half-second before someone
commits. Answer the fear at the point of fear, and conversion moves more than
any redesign will.

## The one addition Hormozi's advice doesn't cover: don't be a commodity

From the agency operator, and worth carrying into every build:

> "If your website looks like every other business in your industry, you'll get
> priced like every other business in your industry."

All the conversion tactics in the world still leave you a commodity if the whole
site could belong to any competitor — and commodities compete on price forever.

Your site is the biggest chance you have to show **what makes you different, what
your process is, and the point of view only you have.** In a market full of
identical AI-generated pages, the ones that win still read like a real business
with a real opinion.

**So: convert hard, but don't sand off the thing that makes someone choose you
over the cheaper option down the road.** When applying everything above, never
strip a page down to a generic high-converting template with the personality
removed.

## How to run this skill

When the user brings you a site, page, or draft:

1. **Diagnose above the fold first.** Read only the first screen. Can you state
   what they do and what the visitor gets, in five seconds? If not, that's finding
   number one and everything else waits.
2. **Score the four Value Equation terms.** Which are strong, which are empty?
   Almost always: dream outcome over-worked, perceived likelihood and effort
   ignored.
3. **Find the risk.** What is the unspoken fear that makes this person close the
   tab? Is anything on the page answering it, and is it next to the button?
4. **Name two or three changes.** Ranked, with the reasoning. Explicitly list
   what you're leaving alone.
5. **Pick the one test.** If they can test, say which single change to run this
   week and what to measure.

When the user is building something new, work in the same order: headline and
hero image first, proof and risk-reversal next, everything else after.

> The whole game is conversion. You win it above the fold, you sell the result
> instead of the product, and you kill the risk before they click.
